# Story Bank & Delivery

Great storytellers don't rely on memory in the moment — they build a library of
practiced stories ready for any context. This reference covers how to collect, organize,
rehearse, and deploy stories.

---

## The Story Bank

A story bank is a personal library of stories categorized by type and purpose, so you
can quickly retrieve the right story for any moment — sales call, speech, team meeting,
first impression.

### Why It Matters

- You never want to be improvising a story under pressure
- The same story can be delivered at different lengths (30/60/120 seconds)
- Stories become better and more natural with practice and refinement
- Knowing you *have* the right story removes the hesitation to use it

### Building Your Story Bank

**Step 1 — Mine your life and work for raw material**

For each category below, identify 2–3 candidate stories:

| Category | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Failure stories** | Times you got it badly wrong, embarrassed yourself, or lost something significant |
| **Turning point stories** | Decisions that changed your direction — career, mindset, relationships |
| **Underdog stories** | Times you were told no, underestimated, or had to prove yourself |
| **Mistake-and-learn stories** | Times a specific error taught you something you still use |
| **Collaboration stories** | Times a person or team made something possible that you couldn't alone |
| **Client/customer transformation** | Someone you helped who changed because of your work |
| **Almost-quit stories** | Times you nearly gave up and what kept you going |

**Step 2 — Map each story to a story type**

(See `story-types.md` for full descriptions)

| Story Type | Map to category |
|---|---|
| Connection | Failure, almost-quit, underdog |
| Convincing | Turning point, client transformation, mistake-and-learn |
| Coaching | Mistake-and-learn, failure, collaboration |
| Capturing | Turning point, underdog, almost-quit |

**Step 3 — Write the CART skeleton for each story**

For every story in your bank, write:
- C: 1–2 sentence context
- A: The key scene in P.A.S.T. format
- R: 1–2 sentence resolution
- T: One-sentence takeaway

**Step 4 — Practice 3 length versions**

Every story in your bank should be rehearsed in three lengths:

| Version | When to use | Target length |
|---|---|---|
| 30-second hook | Opening lines, cold outreach, tight formats | 60–90 words |
| 60-second story | Most meetings, sales calls, presentations | 130–170 words |
| 2-minute full story | Speeches, keynotes, longer content | 280–330 words |

The 30-second version is just the hook and the tension — no resolution stated, just implied.
The 60-second version is full CART without deep P.A.S.T. detail.
The 2-minute version is CART + one fully developed P.A.S.T. scene.

## Story Catalog Template

Use this to document each story in your bank:

```
## Story: [Short Title]

Story Type: [Connection / Convincing / Coaching / Capturing]
Best Used For: [e.g., opening a pitch, handling the "why should I trust you" objection]

CART Skeleton:
  C: [1-2 sentence context]
  A: [Key scene summary — what happened, what was at risk]
  R: [How it resolved]
  T: [One-sentence takeaway]

P.A.S.T. Scene (for 2-min version):
  Place: [location]
  Action: [what was happening]
  Speech: [exact quote from the other person]
  Thoughts: [raw internal monologue]

30-sec version: [write it out fully]
60-sec version: [write it out fully]
2-min version: [write it out fully]

Last rehearsed: [date]
Times delivered: [count]
Notes: [what works, what to cut, what to emphasize]
```

## Delivery Principles

The best-written story can still fail in delivery. These principles apply to any format —
spoken, video, written.

### 1. Slow Down at the Peak Moment

The P.A.S.T. scene is where most storytellers rush. Slow down at the exact moment
the tension peaks. Let the audience sit in it. Silence (in spoken delivery) or a line
break (in written delivery) at the height of tension is more powerful than rushing past it.

### 2. Commit to the Emotion

Don't apologize for how something felt. If you were terrified, say "terrified." If you
were embarrassed, let it show. Half-committed emotion is more uncomfortable for the
audience than full commitment to it.

### 3. Vary Pace by Section

| Section | Pace |
|---|---|
| Context | Medium — get through it fast |
| Adversity buildup | Slow — build tension deliberately |
| Peak moment of P.A.S.T. | Slowest — let it land |
| Resolution | Medium-fast — don't drag the payoff |
| Takeaway | Slow again — let it land and sit |

### 4. Practice Out Loud, Not Just In Your Head

Reading a story in your head and saying it out loud are completely different. Stories
need to be spoken to be refined. Record yourself. Listen back. The places where you
stumble are the places where the structure isn't clear yet.

### 5. Rehearse Until You Can Forget the Script

The goal of rehearsal is not memorization — it is *internalization*. When you truly know
the story, you can improvise within it. The structure is fixed; the words can flex.
Memorized stories sound memorized. Internalized stories sound alive.

### 6. One Takeaway Out Loud

In spoken delivery especially, say the takeaway once, clearly, and then stop. Don't
repeat it. Don't add a second lesson. Don't explain why the takeaway matters. Say it.
Let it land. Move on.

## When to Use a Story vs. Direct Communication

Stories are a precision tool, not the default mode for all communication.

**Use a story when:**
- You sense resistance to an idea or change
- You need something to stick long-term in memory
- You need to change behavior or perspective, not just inform
- The audience is emotionally disengaged
- You're building trust with a new person or audience

**Use direct communication when:**
- The audience already agrees and needs information
- Time is extremely limited (under 60 seconds with no room for story)
- The request is purely logistical or factual
- The relationship has high trust and rapport already established

**Rule of thumb:** 90% of communication should be direct. Stories are the 10% that make
the 90% land.

## Story Refinement Loop

Stories get better with repetition. After every delivery, answer:

1. Where did I rush?
2. Where did the audience disengage?
3. Which line landed best?
4. What would I cut if I had 20% less time?
5. What one word or phrase should I change?

Refine after 3–5 deliveries. The best version of any story emerges through iteration,
not perfection at first draft.
